# Jennings 8 — Part 1 Research: Before the Eight

## Purpose

Part 1 should explain **why Jennings was the kind of place where these murders became so difficult to untangle** before introducing the first victim.

This is not a "corrupt town" conclusion. The episode should establish:
- the size and geography of Jennings;
- class / neighborhood divisions;
- the South Jennings drug / sex-work network;
- the Boudreaux Inn;
- law-enforcement structure;
- how victims were initially framed publicly;
- the Leonard Crochet shooting as later investigative context;
- why later journalists began asking whether this was more than a conventional serial-killer case.

The episode should end with Loretta Chaisson-Lewis disappearing in May 2005.

---

## 1. Jennings was a small town

U.S. Census Bureau QuickFacts lists:
- 2010 Census population: **10,383**.

The murders occurred in and around a town of roughly ten thousand people.

Why this matters:
- victims, witnesses, suspects, officers, informants, dealers, family members, and investigators could overlap socially;
- "six degrees of separation" in a city this small can shrink dramatically;
- a person might simultaneously be a witness in one case, a suspect in another, an informant, and a relative or associate of a victim.

Source:
https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/csv/jenningscitylouisiana/POP010210

Status: **PRIMARY DEMOGRAPHIC FACT.**

## 2. South Jennings and class division

Ethan Brown's reporting describes Jennings as sharply divided by class, with the eight women largely coming from poorer South Jennings.

Brown told Vice that the victims were from South Jennings and criticized the early framing of the women primarily through their "high-risk lifestyles."

Use carefully:
- poverty / class context is relevant;
- do not stereotype South Jennings;
- do not imply all residents shared the victims' social networks.

Source:
https://www.vice.com/en/article/jeff-davis-8-louisiana-bayou-jennings-ethan-brown-sex-workers-murder/

## 3. The Boudreaux Inn

The Boudreaux Inn was a Jennings motel that became central in Ethan Brown's reconstruction of the victims' social world.

Simon & Schuster's official description of Brown's book states that:
- many of the women engaged in sex work as a means of survival;
- their activity frequently occurred at the Boudreaux Inn;
- the motel became a focal point in Brown's investigation into drugs, sex work, law enforcement, and the victim network.

Vice described the Boudreaux Inn as:
- a critical site in Jennings' drug and sex trade during the early-to-mid 2000s;
- a location where several of the women were arrested / worked;
- a place where Frankie Richard was also arrested.

## 4. Early victim framing

Contemporary law-enforcement statements repeatedly described victims as living "high-risk" lifestyles.

KPLC's May 2007 coverage of the arrests in Kristen Lopez's murder quoted Sheriff Ricky Edwards describing multiple victims in that language.

Why this matters:
- it shaped public understanding of the women;
- later journalists argued this framing reduced public empathy;
- it may have affected how the cases were prioritized or perceived, though causation must not be assumed without evidence.

Source:
https://www.kplctv.com/story/6528397/two-arrested-in-murder-of-kristen-gary-lopez/

Status:
**CONTEMPORANEOUS OFFICIAL LANGUAGE / DOCUMENTED.**

## 5. The Leonard Crochet shooting — pre-murder context

A later theory examined by Ethan Brown tied the case to the police shooting of **Leonard Crochet**, described in later reporting as a drug dealer who was shot and killed by an officer during a raid in April 2005.

Brown told A&E that during his early years researching the case he was repeatedly told that all or most of the women had been present around the Crochet shooting.

However, Brown later became **less confident in that claim**:
- he said Loretta Chaisson-Lewis had been around the area;
- he said evidence that most of the other women were present was scant.

This is crucial for Docket Unseen.

The Crochet shooting can be presented as:
- a major theory-generation event;
- evidence of how interconnected the victims' social network and law enforcement may have been;
- something witnesses/journalists later tied to motive theories.

It must **not** be narrated as established motive for the eight killings.

Source:
https://www.aetv.com/articles/jeff-davis-8-interview-ethan-brown-murder-in-the-bayou

Status:
**DISPUTED / WEAKENED JOURNALISTIC THEORY.**

Research still needed:
- exact shooting date;
- officer identity;
- official shooting investigation;
- autopsy;
- witnesses;
- whether Loretta was formally documented at/near the scene;
- whether any of the other seven were formally identified as witnesses.

## 6. How law enforcement initially approached the pattern

By September 2008, after six women had been found, Sheriff Ricky Edwards acknowledged arrests had been made in two cases but charges had been dropped because of insufficient evidence.

Source:
https://www.kplctv.com/story/8998071/womans-body-found-in-local-canal/

By December 18, 2008, after seven deaths, Edwards announced a full-time multi-agency investigative team.

His announcement is important because it said investigators would:
- work each case individually;
- also work the cases collectively;
- determine who was responsible and why.

The wording did not conclusively commit to one killer.

Source:
https://www.kplctv.com/story/9550151/jennings-press-conference-on-mystery-murders/

Status:
**CONTEMPORANEOUS OFFICIAL STATEMENTS.**

## 7. Later common-offender profile

After Necole Guillory became the eighth victim in August 2009, the task force released an offender-behavior profile that repeatedly used the singular term "offender."

This suggested that by that stage a common-offender / serial pattern was a serious working theory.

The task force said the offender likely:
- had significant ties to Jennings / Jefferson Davis Parish;
- knew the area;
- followed media/investigative activity;
- might change routines or transportation after killings.

Important:
This was an **investigative profile**, not proof there was one killer.

## 8. Why the case later shifted away from a simple serial-killer narrative

Ethan Brown's five-year investigation of homicide files led him to argue that:
- multiple offenders may better explain the individual cases;
- victims were connected to informants / law enforcement / suspects in overlapping ways;
- investigative misconduct and conflicts complicated the case;
- a single serial-killer framework may have obscured separate motives.

Simon & Schuster summarizes Brown's work as questioning the original serial-killer framing amid allegations of evidence tampering and police corruption.

Source:
https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Murder-in-the-Bayou/Ethan-Brown/9781982127817

Status:
**JOURNALISTIC THESIS / NOT AN OFFICIAL FINDING.**
